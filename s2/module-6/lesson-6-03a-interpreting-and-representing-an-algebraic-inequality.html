📚 VOCABULARY

Algebraic Inequality: Think of an algebraic inequality like a math sentence that tells you how one number compares to another, but instead of exact numbers, one of the numbers might be a mystery box (which we call a variable, like "x"). The sentence doesn't say they're exactly equal but that one is bigger or smaller than the other. For example, if you have "x > a," it means the mystery box's number is bigger than "a."

Inequality Symbol: These are special signs that tell us how numbers relate to each other. They show if one number is larger, smaller, or possibly equal to another number. The most common symbols are ">" (greater than), "<" (less than), "≥" (greater than or equal to), and "≤" (less than or equal to). For example, ">" means the number on the left side is bigger than the number on the right side.

Number Line: Imagine a long, straight line that goes on forever in both directions. It has a bunch of marks on it, like a ruler, where each mark stands for a different number. Numbers to the right are bigger, and numbers to the left are smaller. We use the number line to show where numbers are located and to help us understand inequalities by showing which numbers are bigger or smaller than others.

Solution Set of an Inequality: When we solve an inequality, like "x > a," we find all the possible numbers that can replace "x" and make the inequality true. The solution set is like a treasure map showing all those possible numbers. On a number line, we mark this set with a circle (open or closed) and a shaded line to show all the numbers that work as solutions.

Variable: A variable is a letter (like "x") used in math to represent a number that we don't know yet. Think of it as a mystery box or a placeholder for a number. In inequalities, we use variables to show that there's a range of numbers that could make the inequality true, not just one specific number.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore defining and understanding the concept of algebraic inequalities in one variable. We will explore how to interpret and represent them on a number line. Algebraic inequalities are like telling a story about numbers - some are bigger, some are smaller, and they all have a place to fit in the number line world.


Understanding Algebraic Inequalities

An algebraic inequality tells us how numbers relate to each other. Instead of saying two numbers are exactly the same, an inequality shows if one number is greater than, less than, greater than, or equal to, or less than or equal to another number. We use variables like "x" to represent our mystery number and symbols like ">", "<", "≥", and "≤" to show the relationship between that number and others.

Symbols and Their Meanings:

  • ">" means greater than
  • "<" means less than
  • "≥" means greater than or equal to
  • "≤" means less than or equal to

How to Represent Inequalities on a Number Line

Open Circle:

  • Use an open circle when your inequality is either ">" (greater than) or "<" (less than).
  • What it means: The number at the open circle is not included in the solution. It's like saying, "You can get close, but you can't be exactly this number."
  • Example: If your inequality is "x > 3," you'd place an open circle at 3 on the number line and shade everything to the right, indicating that x can be any number greater than 3 but not 3 itself.

Closed Circle:

  • Use a closed circle when your inequality includes "≥" (greater than or equal to) or "≤" (less than or equal to).
  • What it means: The number where you've drawn the closed circle is included in the solution. This is like saying, "This number and anything beyond it (to the right for ≥ or to the left for ≤) works for us."
  • Example: If your inequality is "x ≤ -2," you'd put a closed circle at -2 on the number line and shade everything to the left, showing that x can be -2 or any number less than -2.

Now, let's consider four other examples and explain how to represent them.


Example 1

Represent the inequality \(x<-3\) on a number line.

  • Draw a number line, place an open circle at -3 (because -3 is not included), and shade to the left of -3.
  • You can also pick a number 1 unit to the left and right of -3 to test the inequality. Like -2 and -4.
    • Is \(-2<-3\), false
    • Is \(-4<-3\), true. So we shade the line towards -4 or the left.


Example 2

Represent the inequality \(x\geq -7\) on a number line.

  • Draw a number line, place a closed circle at -7 (because -7 is included), and shade to the right of -7.
  • You can also pick a number 1 unit to the left and right of -7 to test the inequality. Like -8 and -6.
    • Is \(-8\geq -7\), false
    • Is \(-6\geq -7\), true. So we shade the line towards -6 or the right.


Example 3

Represent the inequality \(x\leq 5\) on a number line.

  • Draw a number line, place a closed circle at 5 (because 5 is included), and shade to the left of 5.
  • You can also pick a number 1 unit to the left and right of 5 to test the inequality. Like 4 and 6.
    • Is \(4\leq 5\), true. So, we shade towards 4 or to the left.
    • Is \(6\leq 5\), false.


Conclusion

We explored different types of inequalities, using both positive and negative numbers, whole numbers, and decimals. By understanding the symbols and how to graph these inequalities, we can see the range of possible solutions.
