📚 VOCABULARY

Numerical Data: This type of data represents quantities or amounts. It can be counted or measured and is expressed using numbers. For example, the number of books a student reads in a month or the height of students in a class are numerical data.

Statistical Question: A question that can be answered by collecting data that vary among individuals or items. These questions typically lead to answers based on data analysis. For example, "How many minutes do sixth-grade students spend reading each day?" is a statistical question because the answer will vary from student to student.

Statistical Measures: These are calculations used to summarize or describe a set of numerical data. Common statistical measures include mean (average), median (middle value), mode (most frequent value), and range (difference between the highest and lowest values).

Survey: A method of collecting data by asking people questions. Surveys can be conducted in various ways, such as in person, over the phone, or online. They are used to gather information about people's opinions, experiences, or behaviors.

Variability: This refers to how spread out or how different the data points in a data set are from each other and from the average. High variability means that the data points are very spread out, while low variability means they are more similar to each other. For example, if all students in a class have nearly the same number of siblings, the variability in the number of siblings would be low.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will be exploring what a statistical question is. We will look at examples of statistical and non-statistical questions and discuss the differences and importance of statistical questions. Statistical questions help us to gather data to analyze trends and things like average temperatures. A statistical question is one that can be answered by collecting data and where the answer involves variability. Unlike a question with a single, definitive answer, a statistical question expects a range of answers. This lesson will guide you through understanding what makes a question statistical and how to come up with your own statistical questions.

What Makes a Question Statistical?

A statistical question is one that:

  1. Involves variability: The data collected from the question will produce a range of possible values and not just a single answer.
  2. Can be answered by collecting data: You need to gather information from a group of individuals or objects.
  3. Leads to further investigation: The answer to the question allows for analysis and interpretation of the data collected.


What is Variability?

Variability refers to the extent to which data points in a set differ from each other and from the overall average of the set. In simpler terms, it's a measure of how much the numbers in a group vary or spread out. Variability can be seen in many aspects of daily life and is a key concept in statistics because it helps us understand the diversity or uniformity within a set of data.


How Variability Affects a Question Being Statistical

A statistical question is one that anticipates answers based on data that show variability. This means that for a question to be considered statistical, it must be expected to have different answers when asked in different situations or among different groups of people or items. The presence of variability in the potential answers is what makes a question truly statistical.

  1. Presence of Variability: A question that leads to answers with variability is statistical because it requires data collection and analysis to understand the range, trends, or patterns in the responses. For example, asking "How many books do students in 6th grade read per month?" is a statistical question because the number of books will vary from one student to another.

  2. Lack of Variability: On the other hand, a question that does not involve variability and has the same answer every time it is asked is not considered statistical. For instance, "What is the capital city of France?" is not a statistical question because the answer is always "Paris," showing no variability.


Statistical Questions:

  • How many hours do students in my school sleep on a school night?
  • What is the average height of a 6th-grade student in my class?
  • How many books do students in my grade read per month?

Non-Statistical Questions:

  • What is the capital of France?
  • How many minutes are in an hour?
  • What is the height of the tallest building in our city?


Example 1

Review the following questions and decide if they are statistical or non-statistical:

  1. What is the most popular sport in our school?
  2. How many continents are there in the world?
  3. What is the average time students spend on homework each night?


Answers:

  1. Statistical - you would need to survey a variety of students and not all students will have the same answer. Then you can calculate statistical measures like mode.
  2. Non-statistical - No matter who you ask, there is one definitive answer to this question.
  3. Statistical - Again, you need to ask a variety of students and everyone's answer will vary some. It will lead to the collection of data that can then be analyzed.

 

Try Creating Your Own Statistical Questions

Think about what interests you, whether it's related to sports, reading habits, technology use, or any other area. Try to come up with at least three statistical questions you could answer by collecting data from your classmates or family.

 

Conclusion:

Identifying and creating statistical questions is a crucial skill in understanding and analyzing the world around us. By focusing on questions that require data collection and involve variability, we can uncover interesting trends and patterns in our lives and the environment. Keep practicing, and soon you'll be a pro at coming up with insightful statistical questions!

Reflection:

Think about the statistical questions you've created. How would you go about collecting the data to answer them? What kind of answers do you expect to find, and why?
