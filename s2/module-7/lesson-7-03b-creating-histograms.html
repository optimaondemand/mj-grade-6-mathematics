📚 VOCABULARY

Box Plot: A graphical representation of data that shows the distribution based on a five-number summary. It includes a box that represents the interquartile range and lines (whiskers) that extend to the minimum and maximum values.

Five Number Summary: A set of descriptive statistics that provide information about a dataset. It includes the minimum, first quartile (Q1), median (Q2), third quartile (Q3), and maximum.

Frequency: The number of times a data point appears in a dataset. In a histogram, it's represented by the height of the bars.

Histogram: A type of graph that shows the frequency of data within equal intervals or bins. It's used to visualize the distribution of numerical data.

Interquartile Range (IQR): The difference between the third quartile (Q3) and the first quartile (Q1) in a dataset. It measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

Intervals (Bins): The equal divisions or ranges of values in a histogram. Each bin groups a range of data, showing how many data points fall within that range.

Minimum: The smallest value in a dataset.

Maximum: The largest value in a dataset.

Numerical Data: Data that consists of numbers. It can be discrete (countable) or continuous (measurable).

Outlier: A data point that is significantly different from the other data points in a dataset. Outliers can be much higher or lower than the rest of the data.

Quartiles: Values that divide a dataset into four equal parts. The first quartile (Q1) is the median of the lower half of the data, the second quartile (Q2) is the median of the dataset, and the third quartile (Q3) is the median of the upper half of the data.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we're going to explore a fascinating way to visualize data called a histogram. A histogram is a type of graph that shows us how many times or how frequently data values fall into certain ranges or intervals. These ranges are called bins, and they help us see the shape of the data, including where it piles up and where it's spread out. Let's dive into how we create a histogram and what we can learn from it.


Let's begin by understanding what a histogram is.

What is a Histogram?

A histogram looks a bit like a bar chart, but instead of showing separate categories on the x-axis, it shows continuous data intervals. Each bar's height shows how many data points fall within that interval, making it easy to see patterns, such as which ranges are most or least common.


Now, let's explore some examples of building a histogram.

How to Create a Histogram

  1. Collect Your Data: You need a set of numerical data to start with. This could be anything measurable, like heights, scores, or times.

  2. Decide on Your Bins: Bins are the ranges you divide your data into. You want to make sure these are evenly sized - for example, 0-10, 11-20, 21-30, and so on. The range you choose depends on your data's spread and what you find most helpful.

  3. Count the Data Points in Each Bin: Go through your data and count how many values fall into each of your bins.

  4. Draw Your Histogram: On the x-axis, put your bins. On the y-axis, put the count of data points. Then, draw a bar for each bin that reaches up to the count for that bin.


Example 1

You've been tracking how many steps you take each day for 20 days. You want to create a histogram for your data so that you can observe any trends or commonalities in your daily steps.

Your data set is .

  • Let's look at the minimum number of steps and our maximum number to determine our range.
    • Min = 3400
    • Max = 7500
  • We will use this information to determine that we need intervals that range from 3000 to 8000.
  • We can use bin intervals of 1000
    • 3000 - 3999
    • 4000 - 4999
    • 5000 - 5999
    • 6000 - 6999
    • 7000 - 7999
  • Next, we count how many times a number in each interval appears to determine the frequency or "height" of the bar for each interval.
  • You can build a table and use tally marks as you work through the data set.
    • 3000-3999 = IIIII = 5
    • 4000-4999 = IIII = 4
    • 5000-5999 = III = 3
    • 6000-6999 = IIIII I = 6
    • 7000-7999 = II = 2
  • Now, we can create our histogram. Along the horizontal axis, we put our bins. Along the vertical axis, we start at 0 and can go up to 7 or 8 and label it frequency. Then we draw a bar for each bin up to the frequency we counted.

Notice that the axis is divided by the 1000 marks we set for interval length. The bins are what we all left-fill, where we use the number to the left to start and do not include the number to the right in that interval. So, when we look at the bar between 3000 and 4000, it is including all data values between 3000 and 3999. If we had a 4000, it would be counted in the second bar, not the first one.

You can switch it around and have them be right-fill, where the number to the right is included in that bar, but not the number to the left. Then the interval would become 3001 to 4000 for the first bar.

Always determine if you are doing right-fill or left-fill when creating your axis intervals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, histograms are a powerful tool in the world of mathematics and statistics, providing us with a visual representation of how data is distributed across different intervals or bins. Through the process of creating histograms, we've learned how to organize data into these bins, count the frequency of data points within each bin, and then represent these counts visually with bars. This method allows us to quickly grasp the shape of the data's distribution, identify patterns such as skewness or uniformity, and spot outliers that may require further investigation.

From the examples we explored, it's clear that histograms can be applied to a wide range of real-world situations, from tracking daily steps to understanding reading habits or analyzing weather patterns. By examining the height and spread of the bars in a histogram, we can infer the central tendency and variability of the data, even though these specific statistics aren't directly shown on the graph.

The beauty of histograms lies in their simplicity and the depth of insight they can provide into a data set. They help transform raw numbers into a story that can be understood at a glance, making them an essential skill for anyone looking to analyze and communicate data effectively. Remember, the key to creating effective histograms is choosing appropriate bin sizes and ranges that best highlight the characteristics of your data. With practice, you'll become more adept at selecting these parameters and interpreting the resulting graphs. So, keep experimenting with different data sets, and enjoy the insights and stories your histograms reveal. Happy charting, young mathematicians!
