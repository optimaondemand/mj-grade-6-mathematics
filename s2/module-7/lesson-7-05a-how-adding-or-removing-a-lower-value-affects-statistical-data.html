📚 VOCABULARY

Data: Information collected in numbers. Think of it as a collection of facts, like how many books you read last month or the scores you got on your math tests.

Interquartile Range (IQR): This is a way to see how spread out the middle part of your data is. Imagine you line up all your data from smallest to largest, then cut the list into four equal parts. The IQR is the difference between the third part (the third quartile) and the first part (the first quartile). It shows you where the "middle half" of your data lies.

Mean: This is what most people think of as the average. You add up all the numbers in your data and then divide by how many numbers there are. For example, if you have test scores of 80, 90, and 100, the mean would be .

Measures of Center: These are ways to find the middle or the average of your data. The mean and the median are both measures of center because they both give you a way to see what's typical for your data.

Measures of Variability: These help you understand how much your data changes. For example, if all your test scores are close to 90, there's not a lot of variability. But if your scores are all over the place, from 60 to 100, then there's a lot of variability. Range and interquartile range are examples of measures of variability.

Median: The middle number in your data when you line up all the numbers from smallest to largest. If you have an even number of data points, the median is the average of the two middle numbers. For example, in the data 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7, the median is 4.

Outlier: A number in your data that is much higher or much lower than most of the other numbers. It stands out because it's not like the others. For example, if most of your test scores are around 70, but you have one score of 100, that 100 might be an outlier.

Range: The difference between the largest number and the smallest number in your data. It shows you how spread out your numbers are. If your highest test score is 100 and your lowest is 60, the range would be 40.

Skewness: This tells us if our data leans more towards the high numbers or the low numbers. If most of your data is bunched up at the lower end, it's "skewed to the left." If it's bunched up at the higher end, it's "skewed to the right."

Statistical Measures: These are different numbers, like the mean, median, range, and others, that help us understand our data better. They can tell us what's typical, how spread out our numbers are, and whether there are any numbers that stand out a lot.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Welcome to today's math adventure, where we'll explore how adding or subtracting values affects our data, specifically focusing on the mean and median (our measures of center) and the range (our measure of variation). We'll dive into different scenarios to see how these statistics change, especially when the values we're adding or subtracting are lower than the average but not necessarily the lowest values in the set. Let's get started with some real-world examples!


Let's first look at the effects adding or removing values can have on the statistical measures for the data set.

How Addition or Subtraction Affects the Mean

  • Adding a Value: When you add a new data value to your set, the mean (average) will adjust based on the overall sum of the data. If the new value is lower than the current mean but higher than the minimum, the mean will decrease, but not as dramatically as if you were adding a value lower than the current minimum.

  • Subtracting a Value: Removing a data value that's lower than the mean but not the minimum will cause the mean to increase slightly. This is because you're taking away a value that was pulling the average down, but not removing the lowest value which has a stronger pull.

Impact on the Median with Addition or Subtraction

  • Median Adjustments: The median may or may not change with the addition or subtraction of a data value, depending on where the new or removed value falls within the ordered data set. If the value is below the median, the median's position might shift, but the impact is more nuanced than on the mean. For a single addition or subtraction, the median could shift to the next data point closer to the center if the data set's size changes from odd to even, or vice versa.

Understanding Changes to the Range

  • Range Considerations: Adding or subtracting a value lower than the measures of center but not the minimum will generally not affect the range of the data set, as the range is determined by the difference between the maximum and minimum values. Unless the added or subtracted value becomes the new minimum or maximum, the range remains unchanged.

  • New Minimum: Introducing a new minimum value to your data set directly affects the range, as the range is the difference between these two values. Adding a lower minimum increases the range, showing greater variation within your data.

  • Removing an Extreme Value: Eliminating the current minimum value decreases the range if there's no identical value to take its place. This indicates a decrease in the spread of your data, suggesting the data points are becoming more closely packed around the center.
