📖 PRIOR KNOWLEDGE

In previous lessons and grades, you learned about the minimum, maximum, and median of a set of data. As we get ready to explore creating box plots, it is important to review these statistical measures as they are the beginning of building a box plot and the five-number summary that creates the box plot. These measures help us understand the range and the center of a data set. Let's break down what each term means and then practice finding them with some real-world examples.


What Do They Mean?

  • Minimum: This is the smallest number in your data set. Imagine you're lining up for a race; the person at the very front of the line is like the minimum value, the lowest or first in order.

  • Maximum: Opposite to the minimum, this is the largest number in your data set. Back to our race, the person at the end of the line, who might be the tallest, is like the maximum value, the highest or last in order.

  • Median: This is the middle value of your data set when the numbers are arranged in order. If you were picking a team captain from a line of people based on height, the person right in the middle would be your pick. If there's an even number of values, you take the average of the two middle numbers.

Why Are They Important?

Understanding the minimum and maximum gives us a sense of the data's range – how spread out the values are. The median helps us find the center or the "middle ground" of the data, which is useful for seeing what's typical or common.


Example 1

Situation: A basketball coach is recording the heights of players on a school team. The heights (in inches) are: 58, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 65, 66, 67, 68, 70.

Finding the Measures:

  • Minimum: Look for the smallest height.
  • Maximum: Find the tallest height.
  • Median: Since there are 12 values, we find the middle by averaging the 6th and 7th heights.

Answers & Interpretation:

  • Minimum: 58 inches
  • Maximum: 70 inches
  • Median: (64 + 65) / 2 = 64.5 inches

This tells us that the heights of the players range from 58 to 70 inches, with the middle height around 64.5 inches. The distribution here is fairly normal, with most players being close to the median height.


Example 2

Situation: A group of friends discusses their weekly allowances (in dollars): 5, 5, 5, 10, 10, 10, 15, 15, 15, 20, 25, 25, 100.

Finding the Measures:

  • Minimum: The lowest allowance.
  • Maximum: The highest allowance.
  • Median: With 13 values, the median will be the 7th value so that there are 6 values below and 6 values above making it the middle value.

Answers & Interpretation:

  • Minimum: $5
  • Maximum: $100
  • Median: $15

The minimum and maximum show us a huge range in allowances, mainly because of the outlier ($100), which is much higher than the rest. The median tells us that half of the friends get $15 or less for their weekly allowance, indicating that despite the outlier, most allowances are on the lower end, demonstrating a skewed distribution.


Conclusion

These examples show how minimum, maximum, and median can give us a quick snapshot of our data, helping us understand the range of values and what's typical within a set. By practicing with real-world data, you'll get better at spotting these measures and what they reveal about the world around you. Keep exploring, and soon you'll be a data wizard!
