📖 PRIOR KNOWLEDGE

In previous lessons, you learned about the statistical measures mean, median, mode, and range. You learned how to find these measures and what they mean in context of the situation for the data. As we get ready to explore how adding or removing data values to a set of data affects the statistical measures, it is important to review what the measures are and how to find them.

What is Mean?

The mean is just another word for the average. You can find it by adding up all the numbers in your list and then dividing by how many numbers there are. It gives us a good idea of what's typical for our data, or where the middle ground is.

What is Median?

The median is the middle number in a list that's been arranged from smallest to largest. If there's an even number of items in the list, then the median is the average of the two middle numbers. The median is super useful because it shows us the exact middle point of our data, even if some numbers are really big or really small.

Why Are The Mean and Median Important?

The mean and median give us clues about the data. The mean shows us the overall level, but it can be influenced by very high or very low numbers. The median gives us a middle point that isn't affected by extreme values, which can be really helpful to understand what's normal or typical in the data.

Let's explore some examples.


Example 1

Situation: A local ice cream shop keeps track of how many ice creams are sold each day for two weeks. The numbers are: 12, 15, 9, 13, 14, 17, 11, 10, 16, 12, 14, 15, 13, 18.

Question: What are the mean and median sales, and what do they tell us about ice cream sales over these two weeks?

  • Finding the mean
    • Add all the numbers together and divide by the number of days sales were tracked.
  • Finding the median
    • Arrange the numbers in ascending order and find the middle number.

The mean tells us the average number of ice creams sold in a day is 13.5. The median tells us that are typical number of ice cream sales is 13.5 as well. Since these two numbers are identical, the data set is fairly uniform with no skewness or outliers that would affect the average number.


Example 2

Situation: A group of friends tracks how many books they read over the summer: 8, 7, 2, 3, 6, 3, 10, 7, 8, 11, 8, 9, 9, 2.

Question: What are the mean and median number of books read, and what do these measures tell us about the friends' reading habits?

  • Finding the mean
    • Add all the numbers together and divide by the number of friends. Since we divide by 14, we will need to round the number. Round off to the nearest hundredths.
  • Finding the median
    • Arrange the numbers in ascending order and find the middle number.

The mean tells us the average number of books read is about 6.64. The median tells us that the typical number of books read was 7.5. These numbers are off. Telling us that our data is skewed some. There were a couple who only read 2 or 3 books when we know the most typical number of books read was between 7 and 8 since our median is 7.5.

What are Mode and Range?

Mode is the number that appears most frequently in a set of numbers. It's like the favorite ice cream flavor among your friends; the one most people choose is the "mode" flavor.

Range shows the difference between the highest and lowest numbers in a set. It tells us how spread out the numbers are, kind of like measuring the distance between the first and last runner in a race.


Why Are They Important?

The mode helps us understand what's common or popular in a group of numbers. When we talk about the range, we're looking at how varied or similar the numbers are. Together, these measures give us clues about trends and variability in data.

Conclusion

These examples show that mean and median help us understand different aspects of our data. The mean gives a general idea, while the median helps us see what's typical when we ignore the extremes. Whether it's ice cream sales, summer reading, or marathon times, these measures of central tendency can tell us a lot about what's going on in the world around us. Happy calculating!

These examples demonstrate how mode and range offer additional insights into our data, complementing what we learn from mean and median. The mode helps us spot trends or commonalities, while the range gives us a snapshot of the data's spread. By combining these measures, we can paint a fuller picture of what's happening in any given situation, from ice cream sales and summer reading to marathon running times.
